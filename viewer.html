How the Flood Simulation Works

Bowling Green, KY · Warren County · GPU-accelerated hydrodynamic model · TACC Vista

1. Why Bowling Green Floods Differently

Bowling Green sits on karst limestone terrain — a landscape riddled with underground caves, sinkholes, and internal drainage. When rain falls, water doesn't just run downhill toward streams: it also drains into sinkholes, collects in closed depressions, and can overwhelm underground conduits.

The city also sits at the confluence of Barren River, Lost River (which disappears underground), and numerous unnamed karst tributaries — making flood prediction especially complex.

2. HPC Flood Simulation (TACC Vista)

The time-animated flood layers are produced by a 2D GPU flood solver implementing the Local Inertial Approximation (Bates et al., 2010) — the same numerical scheme used by LISFLOOD-FP. Run on TACC Vista's NVIDIA H100 Grace-Hopper nodes via CuPy, processing 80 million grid cells per timestep at 2-meter resolution across a 10×10 km domain.

3. Rainfall Input (SCS Type II)

Each Vista run applies a 24-hour rainfall of the NOAA Atlas 14 depth for its return period (2-year 3.5 in up to 100-year 7.6 in, Warren County), distributed in time with the same cumulative curve for every storm (the heaviest rain falls around hours 10–14). Use the Storm menu to switch runs. The 2-year run is shown for hours 1–11; the others for all 24 hours. Cumulative rainfall and the rate for each hour are shown in the header.

4. FEMA Flood Zones

The red/orange FEMA overlay is the official National Flood Hazard Layer (NFHL) for Warren County:

  • AE (red) — High risk; BFE established. Flood insurance mandatory for federally backed mortgages.
  • A (orange) — High risk; no detailed BFE study.
  • X (yellow) — Moderate/minimal risk; 0.2% annual chance flood zone.

7. The Fluid Flow View

The Fluid flow mode shows the simulated water as a moving surface instead of flat coloured polygons. It is drawn by your graphics card from flow grids (one picture of the flood per simulated hour) and blends smoothly between hours as time plays.

  • Depth colour: the solver saved water depth only, as four tiers (0.1–0.3, 0.3–0.6, 0.6–1.2 and over 1.2 m). Colour goes from pale blue (shallow) to dark blue (deep). Nothing below 0.1 m is shown.
  • How speed and direction are obtained: the solver did not save velocities. They are derived afterwards from the slope of the water surface (water level = ground height + depth): water runs downhill along that surface, and its speed follows from the slope and the depth with Manning’s equation for open-channel flow. They are therefore an estimate of the flow, not the solver’s own output.
  • Streaks: thin lines that drift with the flow. Direction is the way the water goes; colour runs from pale cyan (slow) to white (fast, about 1 m/s and above).
  • Arrows: point the way the water flows; a longer arrow means faster water, and amber arrows mark speeds above about 2 m/s.
  • Drainage paths: optional overlay of where flowing water collects into channels (cumulative discharge).
  • Preview or full precision: a badge shows what the grids were built from. Preview from depth tiers is rebuilt from the four-tier polygons: depth is the smoothest surface that fits the tiers, so levels are only known to about 0.1 m. Full-precision grids come from the solver’s depth rasters and show the real depth values.

Limits. Derived speeds are indicative only. Very flat water (ponds, the river, wide floodplain) is drawn at rest because its slope cannot be resolved. Karst drainage, sinkholes and underground conduits are not modelled by the velocity method, so flow into sinkholes is not shown and water near them can appear to pond or move in ways it would not underground.
